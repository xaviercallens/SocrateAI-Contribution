# What's new, 28 October 2026

*For readers who are not scientists. No formulas.*

Seven things were published in the last few days. None of them is a big discovery. Several are corrections, a replication, or "we looked and found nothing". That is what most honest research looks like, and it is the reason this page exists.

**Two things apply to every item below.**

- **AI-checked, not human-checked.** The work was done by AI agents under my direction. Each paper was then examined by a *different* AI agent, which redid the calculations with its own programs and asked for corrections. The corrections were applied. No human scientist has reviewed any of it.
- **The levels.** As explained in [Elenchus in ten minutes](elenchus-in-10-minutes.md), each claim carries a level. **A**: the Lean proof checker looked at every step. **L**: it is in a serious published source. **C**: someone reasoned or computed exactly, but no machine checked the whole thing. **X**: a rough calculation with rounded numbers. A paper is only as strong as its weakest link.

## 1. A complete list of eleven-point "amplifier" networks: there are exactly four, and we already knew them

**The question.** Take a network of 11 points joined by links. Some networks help a useful mutation spread through a population a little more than chance would. They are called amplifiers. How many 11-point networks are amplifiers?

**What we did.** We went through all 1,006,700,565 possible connected networks of 11 points, one by one, and decided each with an exact rule.

**What we found.** Exactly four are amplifiers. All four had already been published by another researcher, Richter, in 2022. The only new thing is the word *exactly*: there are no others.

**Level.** The census itself is level C (an exact computation, not machine-checked as a whole). The proof checker confirmed the claim for three of the four networks (level A for those statements).

**What this does NOT show.** Nothing about networks with more than 11 points, about other kinds of networks, or about real populations.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23722593](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23722593)

## 2. A correction of our own earlier statement about this

**What happened.** Our earlier paper on networks with up to 10 points also looked at one 11-point network and presented it as *the* 11-point example. That was wrong: there are four, and ours was only the first of them. We published a corrected version (v1.1) that says so. No number and no proof changed. The mistake was in how we described it.

**Level.** Unchanged from the earlier paper: level C for the census, level A for one Lean statement.

**What this does NOT show.** The correction does not add any new result. It removes a misleading sentence.

## 3. A replication of a computation about ten runners

**The puzzle.** Several runners circle a track at different constant speeds. The *lonely runner conjecture* says that each runner will, at some moment, be far from all the others. Nobody has proved it in general. Other researchers reduced the case of 11 runners to a very large but finite computation.

**What we did.** We wrote our own program, without reading theirs, and ran the same computation. It gives the same answer as theirs at every one of the 61 steps.

**What we found.** The same result as the original authors. This is a **replication**, not a new theorem. It makes their computation a little more trustworthy. It does not settle the conjecture.

**Level.** C: exact whole-number arithmetic, not machine-checked as a whole.

**What this does NOT show.** It does not prove the lonely runner conjecture. Our "independence" is limited: the same kind of AI system wrote our program, even though it did not read theirs.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23726067](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23726067)

## 4. A crystal-lattice energy: the triangular arrangement is *not* a minimum

**The setting.** Imagine points that repel each other, arranged in a flat regular pattern. For resting points, the triangle pattern is known to have the lowest energy for many kinds of repulsion. In the quantum world points keep trembling, and the trembling carries an energy of its own.

**What we found.** For seven models of the repulsion, we showed with guaranteed-error calculations that gently shearing the triangle pattern *lowers* the trembling energy. So the triangle pattern is not a minimum of that energy. We had hoped to find at least one case where it is a minimum. We did not.

**Level.** The logic and the exact sign checks are level A. The numerical bounds that feed them are level C, so the paper as a whole is C. The reviewing agent flagged its own check as provisional.

**What this does NOT show.** Nothing about real materials, real layers of particles, or melting. It concerns a simplified model in model units, not a prediction for a laboratory.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23721616](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23721616)

## 5. Carbon capture from the air: one assumption decides the answer

**The question.** A published model of capturing carbon dioxide from the air concluded that it is greatly beneficial to start the cycle cold. We asked whether this survives when every form of heat is counted according to how useful it really is, and when cooling is paid for.

**What we found.** It depends. A cold start still helps if no heat is recovered. But how much it helps, and whether it helps at all when heat is fully recovered, depends on one thing: whether you count the energy needed to chill the incoming air. The published model left that out. With it counted, the answer changes.

**Level.** A for some small Lean statements, X for most of the numerical scans, C for the rest.

**What this does NOT show.** Nothing about any real material, machine or plant, and nothing about the climate. It is about how a model is accounted for.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23721326](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23721326)

## 6. An idealised plasma model: one fuel cannot ignite unless the electrons are cooler than the ions

**The setting.** In a deliberately simplified textbook model of a hot gas, we asked when a mixture of hydrogen and boron could sustain its own burning.

**What we found.** If electrons and ions are at the same temperature, there is no window in which it can: the energy lost as light is too large in this model. Burning is possible only if the electrons are kept noticeably cooler than the ions, and we give bounds on how much cooler.

**Level.** C: guaranteed-error calculations, not replayed in Lean. Two small statements are level A.

**What this does NOT show.** This is an idealised model. It is **not about reactors**, and says nothing about any real device, any engineering, or whether such a device could ever be built. The bounds hold only for the two reaction-rate formulas used.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23733098](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23733098)

## 7. A clarification note on our audit of OpenAI's Navier-Stokes Lean files

**Background.** OpenAI published a large collection of Lean files about the equations of fluid flow. Earlier we rebuilt it from scratch and audited it. This is a new version of that audit with no change of result.

**What the note says.** It spells out exactly which statements were audited, what each was judged against, and that we read one related preprint directly rather than through a second-hand summary.

**Level.** Unchanged: the rebuild and the proof checks are level A for four statements, with a number of level C and X items, as before.

**What this does NOT show.** It is **not** a claim that a famous prize problem is solved, and it does not verify that OpenAI's proofs are right beyond the checks written in the paper. The credit for those files belongs to their authors.
