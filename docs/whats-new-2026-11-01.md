# What's new, 1 November 2026

[Version française](whats-new-2026-11-01.fr.md) · Previous page: [What's new, 28 October 2026](whats-new-2026-10-28.md)

*For readers who are not scientists. No formulas.*

Eleven more things were published between 31 October and 1 November. None of them is a big discovery. **Several turned out not to be new at all, and we say so.** Part of the method is to look, and then to report honestly that someone else got there first, or that the result was already classical, or that we found nothing. That is most of what this page contains.

**Two things apply to every item below.**

- **AI-checked, not human-checked.** The work was done by AI agents under my direction. Each paper was then examined by a *different* AI agent, which redid the calculations with its own programs and asked for corrections. The corrections were applied. No human scientist has reviewed any of it.
- **The levels.** As explained in [Elenchus in ten minutes](elenchus-in-10-minutes.md), each claim carries a level. **A**: the Lean proof checker looked at every step (and even then, nobody has checked that the statement means what we say it means). **L**: it is in a serious published source. **C**: someone reasoned or computed exactly, but no machine checked the whole thing. **X**: a rough calculation with rounded numbers. A paper is only as strong as its weakest link.

**A quick guide to the eleven.** Not new, recomputed or rechecked: items 4, 9, 10. Model or machine-check work where the main fact was already known: items 5, 6, 7, 8. Partly new but small: items 1, 11. Our own question, answered in a tiny case: item 2. A statement about a data file, not about materials: item 3.

## 1. Runners on a track: a conjecture fails for 18 to 38 runners (and stays open from 39 to 94)

**The puzzle.** Runners circle a track at different constant speeds, and each starts from its own chosen spot. A "shifted" version of the lonely runner puzzle claims that some moment always exists when every runner is at least a certain distance from its own marker on the track. Other researchers had already shown that this claim is false for 5 to 17 runners, and again for 95 runners or more. Nobody had published a case in between.

**What we did.** For every number of runners from 18 to 38 we give an explicit example, with exact fractions, in which the claim fails. Anyone can check it with a small program. Three different checkers agreed, one of them written by the reviewing agent.

**What we found.** The claim is false for each of those 21 numbers. For 39 to 94 runners we searched within a limited budget and found nothing; that range is **open**. We did not search at all for 61 runners and beyond.

**Level.** C for the examples (exact computation). The Lean proof checker looked at the key statement for two sample cases, and the reviewing agent ran it for all of them (level A), but nobody has yet "locked" that statement against what the paper says it means.

**What this does NOT show.** It does not show that the claim fails for every number of runners. It says nothing about the original lonely runner puzzle. The novelty comes from a literature check on two dates, with some risk that we missed something; we make no priority claim. We have not contacted the authors of the earlier papers.

## 2. Our own guess about a sum, checked on every case of the smallest size

**The setting.** In an earlier paper we guessed that a certain quantity built from a pattern of +1 and -1 signs never exceeds 3. If true for all inputs, it would pin down a constant we care about.

**What we did.** For the smallest case, we checked every one of the 281 trillion possible sign patterns (2 to the power 48). Three separate programs, one of them written by the reviewing agent in a different way, gave the same table of results.

**What we found.** The quantity never exceeds 3. It reaches 3 exactly, many times. As a sanity test, replacing the 3 by 4 immediately produces violations, so the check can fail when it should.

**Level.** C: an exact computation, with no machine-checked proof.

**What this does NOT show.** It is **our own** guess, so this is not independent confirmation of anything. It covers only signs and only the smallest size. It says nothing about general inputs, nothing about larger sizes, and nothing about the value of the constant. The computers and compilers are trusted.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23840417](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23840417)

## 3. How many stored crystal-stiffness tables in a public database fail a basic test?

**The setting.** A crystal at rest can only hold together if its table of stiffness numbers passes a simple mathematical test. A well-known public database of computed materials stores such tables for tens of thousands of entries.

**What we did.** We ran the test on all 34,323 stored tables, with exact arithmetic so rounding cannot change the answer. For every table that fails, we kept the exact witness showing why. A second method, programmed by the reviewing agent, agreed on every entry.

**What we found.** 11,481 tables, about a third (33.45 %), fail the test as stored. A few hundred also pass an incorrect shortcut test that appears in the literature.

**Level.** C for the full run. Lean checked a dozen sample cases (level A).

**What this does NOT show.** It is a statement about **stored numbers in a file**, not about materials. Many entries are raw computer outputs, including hypothetical or unrelaxed structures, so a high share is expected. It does **not** say the database or its authors are wrong, and it says nothing about any real material in a laboratory. The data are under a licence that asks for credit, and they are not copied here.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23842486](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23842486)

## 4. Networks where every point has three links: a re-check, not a new result

**The question.** Take a network in which every point has exactly three links and which can be split into two teams with links only between teams. Does it always contain a closed loop of length 4, 8 or 16? Up to what size has this been checked?

**What we did.** We recomputed the answer, up to 68 points, by exhaustive search that writes out a certificate for every step. Two kinds of checker, including one written by the reviewing agent, accepted the certificates.

**What we found.** Yes, up to 68 points, every such network has such a loop.

**Level.** C: exact search with checkable certificates, not a Lean proof.

**What this does NOT show.** **It is not new.** Other researchers had claimed more: one reported, without a certificate, a result covering larger sizes; another covered up to 66 points with a solver but without a proof log. The credit and the priority belong to them. All we add is a recomputation with certificates and an independent check. It says nothing about the famous conjecture behind it, nor about more than 68 points.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23846709](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23846709)

## 5. How long until the surface of a battery particle fills up? A model calculation of a classical formula

**The setting.** In a simplified model of a battery particle, a ball with a steady flow of material entering at its surface, we asked when the surface first reaches a given level.

**What we did.** We computed guaranteed bounds for 24 combinations of settings, using parameters printed in a public battery-modelling program. The reviewing agent reproduced them with its own solver. It also found a rounding mistake in our conversion to seconds, which we fixed and re-checked.

**What we found.** The bounds are very tight. But the textbook formula for this situation already gives almost the same answer, so **the certificate adds little**. In the same program, a more detailed model reaches the level earlier than the simple one, and fails to run at high charging rates.

**Level.** C for the bounds, L for the classical formula, X for the comparison between program models.

**What this does NOT show.** Nothing about any real battery, its safety or its charging speed. One set of parameters, one simplified model. The comparison between program models is an observation, not a diagnosis of the program.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23848009](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23848009)

## 6. A simple model of how the air's carbon behaves after emissions: a classical effect, applied to a simple model

**The setting.** In a deliberately simple, linear textbook model of how an added puff of carbon fades away, we studied what happens after emissions stop, and what happens when emissions rise and then fall once.

**What we did.** We proved by hand that the model's excess carbon declines smoothly once emissions stop. We computed guaranteed bounds on the time and size of its peak in 63 scenarios. We showed that, for certain emission shapes, this simple model can have two separate peaks, with a dip between them, and we gave a precise condition.

**What we found.** The two-peak effect is real **in the model**, with certified examples.

**Level.** C for most results. The part about discrete emission amounts was checked in Lean (level A), pending a lock on the statement. Whether the peak is unique is level X.

**What this does NOT show.** The effect itself is classical: it follows from an old mathematical theorem from 1956, which we read only through secondary sources. We add the explicit condition and the worked examples. This is a **linear model only**. It says nothing about the real carbon cycle, real emission paths, amounts of carbon, warming or policy.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23848023](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23848023)

## 7. How much carbon does seawater take up per unit of added alkalinity? A number long known, now with guaranteed bounds

**The setting.** In a textbook model of the chemistry of seawater, we asked how much more dissolved carbon the water holds when its alkalinity is raised by one unit.

**What we did.** We computed guaranteed lower and upper bounds over a range of temperature, saltiness and carbon dioxide level, using the constants as printed in a standard reference guide. The reviewing agent confirmed the bounds and found a tighter one.

**What we found.** The answer lies between about 0.70 and 0.96, close to the value around 0.8 that specialists have known for a long time.

**Level.** C for the bounds (given the constants). Lean checked only the algebra (level A), with its statement not yet locked.

**What this does NOT show.** **The value is not new.** What we add is a guaranteed interval with stated constants. It is chemistry of a simple model only. It is **not** a statement about how much carbon any action would remove, not about the climate, not about ecological safety, and not advocacy for any deployment. We did not read the original papers behind the constants; we relied on the reference guide.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23848689](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23848689)

## 8. An idealised engine model: a small mathematical fact checked by a machine; the main bound was already known

**The setting.** A published simplified model of a heat engine gives an upper limit on its efficiency at a given power, depending on how unequal two losses are. The authors say this limit grows with that inequality, and that a pen-and-paper proof is hard.

**What we did.** We proved in Lean, line by line, that the limit really does grow with the inequality, over the whole allowed range.

**What we found.** It does. The machine confirms it.

**Level.** A for the Lean statements (not yet locked against their meaning). The link between our form and the printed formula is level C.

**What this does NOT show.** **The limit itself was already known**, with proofs by others; only the growth statement lacked a published proof, and only the machine check is new, as far as our non-systematic search shows. It is about an idealised model, not a real engine or power plant, and does not show the limit is reached. Our first copy of the printed formula had an error, which the reviewing agent caught and we corrected.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23855967](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23855967)

## 9. A finite check of two old conjectures that others claim are already proved

**The setting.** Two old conjectures, from a well-known list of computer-generated guesses, relate how many positive and negative values a network's numbers have to its total "energy".

**What we did.** We checked them on all 1,018,690,325 connected networks with 4 to 11 points. Positive and negative tests were included: an old, disproved guess was caught, and two true theorems were not flagged. The reviewing agent recomputed all networks up to 10 points and a bit over 12 % of the 11-point ones.

**What we found.** No counterexample.

**Level.** X for the main scan (rounded numbers), C for the borderline cases decided exactly.

**What this does NOT show.** **This is not new.** Other authors have claimed a proof of both conjectures (not yet refereed); we did not check their proof. A finite check cannot prove anything for all sizes, and it covers only up to 11 points.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23856042](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23856042)

## 10. Someone else's claimed counterexample to an old geometry conjecture: we re-checked the computer part

**The setting.** An author recently posted a 101-point arrangement in space that he says disproves a classical conjecture of Gale about colouring and distances.

**What we did.** We rebuilt the claim with exact arithmetic from the numbers he printed, and checked, with proofs that independent programs confirmed, that the resulting distance network cannot be coloured with four colours. The reviewing agent did the same with its own code.

**What we found.** The computer part of his claim holds up. We also found that the arrangement is fragile: moving one coordinate by a tiny amount makes the colouring possible.

**Level.** C: exact computation with checkable proofs, not Lean.

**What this does NOT show.** **We claim nothing new.** This is a re-check of a result by someone else, and the credit is his. We do not claim to have disproved the conjecture. The last step, from "cannot be coloured" to the stated bound, is argued but not machine-checked. Because the points are another author's, we do not copy them into this repository.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23859706](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23859706)

## 11. Searching for a counterexample to a lonely-runner refinement: none found in the boxes we searched

**The setting.** A refined version of the lonely runner puzzle says something about runners whose best moment of loneliness is nearly the worst allowed.

**What we did.** We listed every such case, exactly, in several boxes of possible speeds, and compared with the authors' own table and a published program. The reviewing agent wrote an independent program to re-screen every box and got identical lists.

**What we found.** No counterexample in the boxes we searched. Part of what we did only repeats the authors' own table; only two of the boxes go beyond what was published.

**Level.** C: exact computation, not machine-checked as a whole.

**What this does NOT show.** It is neither a proof nor a refutation, and says nothing outside the boxes. One box we had planned was not run. A particular case we hit along the way was already in the literature's range and is not a new finding.

[doi:10.5281/zenodo.23859744](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23859744)
